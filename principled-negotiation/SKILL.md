# Principled negotiation

Help the user pursue a workable agreement while understanding whether agreement serves them. This is an independent application of publicly described ideas associated with Getting to Yes and Harvard's Program on Negotiation. The preparation workflow below is an original adaptation.

## Establish the mandate

Identify the parties, issues, relationship, decision authority, and user's desired outcome. Separate stated constraints from preferences and proposals. Use supplied facts before asking for additional details. Ask about a missing limit when it would materially affect a recommended commitment; do not invent one.

Distinguish what each party demands from why it may matter. Label the other party's inferred interests as hypotheses and prepare questions to check them. Treat their reported concerns as evidence about their perspective, not automatic proof that their factual claims are correct.

## Prepare alternatives and standards

Define the user's BATNA: the best feasible course available if this negotiation produces no agreement. It is an alternative the user could actually pursue, not their ideal negotiated outcome. Identify its costs, dependencies, uncertainty, and any practical ways to improve it.

If a reservation point is needed, derive it from the alternative, constraints, and consequences. Keep it separate from an opening proposal or aspiration. For agreements with several terms, assess the package rather than comparing headline price alone. Do not invent competing offers or claim the other party has no alternative.

Identify external criteria that could help evaluate terms, such as relevant benchmarks or agreed performance measures. Verify current factual benchmarks where needed and explain limits in comparability. Criteria should be inspectable; describing an unsupported preference as fair does not establish a standard.

## Develop options and a conversation plan

Create packages that address the parties' relevant interests. Explore differences in timing, risk allocation, scope, or priorities when they create room for an agreement. Distinguish a real tradeoff from an assumed shared preference. Do not claim a bargaining range exists when the other party's limits are unknown.

Prepare a respectful opening, useful discovery questions, a reasoned proposal, and responses to likely objections. Keep anticipated reactions conditional. Make space to disagree about substance without attacking identity or attributing bad faith without evidence.

State what the user can accept, what requires further approval, and what should prompt a pause or withdrawal when the context supports those limits. Respectful negotiation can include a firm refusal. Do not optimize solely for obtaining agreement.

## Draft, practise, or review

Return the requested brief, message, or negotiation plan first. A brief can summarize interests, alternative, criteria, packages, open questions, and decision limits. Keep private limits separate from outward-facing language so they are not accidentally disclosed in a draft to the counterpart.

For role-play, simulate one exchange at a time and allow the counterpart to resist or propose a different package. Mark role-play as simulated. Evaluate the user's questions, reasoning, and commitments rather than praising compliance as the only successful outcome.

For an actual offer, identify ambiguities and compare it with the user's alternative and mandate. Do not interpret an unverified assumption as an agreed term. Drafting and practising do not authorize sending a proposal, accepting terms, or making a commitment.

### Original example

A supplier asks for a larger order and the user wants a smaller commitment. Explore whether the supplier's concern is production setup, demand certainty, or something else before proposing staged deliveries. The supplier's motive remains a hypothesis until checked, and staged delivery may not solve their concern.
