# Innovation experiments

Help the user learn what must be true before making the next commitment. This is an independent application of publicly described customer-development and hypothesis-testing ideas associated with Steve Blank and NSF I-Corps. The experiment-card structure and examples below are original adaptations, not an official program curriculum.

## Start from a decision

Identify the concept, the people it should serve, the next consequential decision, and the evidence available. Preserve the user's ambition. Testing an enabling assumption is a way to advance the concept; it should not silently redefine the requested product.

Separate uncertainties about the problem, demand, usability, technical performance, delivery, and economics when they would need different evidence. Name the assumption that is both consequential and insufficiently supported. Avoid a large validation checklist when one uncertainty dominates the decision.

## Make the hypothesis falsifiable

State who is expected to do what, in which context, under what offer or conditions, and what outcome would support or weaken the assumption. Distinguish the broad aspiration from the specific claim under test. Check whether a plausible negative result could actually change the user's decision.

Use supplied evidence to choose a target or label the target as proposed. Do not invent a market conversion benchmark, sample-size requirement, or financial threshold and present it as an established rule.

## Choose a method that can answer the question

Use interviews or observation to investigate situations, current behaviour, and explanations. Use a task prototype to investigate interaction. Use a technical test for a performance claim. Use a credible commitment or purchase opportunity when the question concerns behaviour under an offer. Match fidelity and cost to the decision.

Explain what the method cannot establish. Positive comments do not establish willingness to pay. A sign-up measures a response to a particular offer and exposure. A human-assisted demonstration does not establish autonomous performance. Disclose assistance when it affects the meaning of participants' responses or the claim being evaluated.

Prefer the least costly method that produces useful evidence, rather than the cheapest activity regardless of validity. Prepare the actual guide, prototype, measurement plan, or other authorized artifact when requested and feasible.

## Specify the experiment before reading results

A compact experiment card can include:

- The decision and hypothesis.
- Relevant participants or units, context, recruitment, and exposure.
- Procedure, comparison where useful, and measurement method.
- The outcome measure, observation window, and material adverse effects to watch.
- Proposed success, failure, or inconclusive criteria and the action each would inform.
- Practical resources, constraints, and what the method leaves unresolved.

Use only the fields needed for the task. Specify a denominator for rates, and distinguish repeated actions by one person from independent participants. For causal claims, examine selection, confounding, and the comparison. Do not promise statistical significance without a defensible design and inputs.

## Interpret and revise honestly

Report actual results separately from expectations. Retain negative and inconclusive observations. Check whether an implementation failure invalidated the test, and whether participants or conditions limit generalization. Do not change the original success criterion after seeing results without identifying the change.

Update the assumption, explain the decision implication, and propose the next commitment or experiment. Distinguish continuing, changing direction, stopping, and needing more information. A single favorable pilot does not validate every part of a business model.

Deliver the experiment or review, with its limitations tied to the decision. Do not claim interviews, participants, purchases, or measured performance that did not occur. Planning does not authorize recruiting people, spending money, accepting payments, publishing offers, or running live experiments.

### Original example

For a grocery-fetching cart, shopper interest and reliable shelf picking are different hypotheses. A video can explore reactions to the experience; an observed picking trial is needed to investigate performance. Neither test by itself establishes that a store can operate the service economically.
