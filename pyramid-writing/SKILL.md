# Pyramid writing

Write so a reader can grasp the main point quickly and examine the reasoning at the depth they need. This is an independent application of Barbara Minto's publicly described methods, not an official Minto or McKinsey skill. The instructions and examples below are original operational adaptations; sources appear at the end.

## Establish the communication task

Identify the reader, the question they need answered, and the decision or action the writing should enable. Use the user's draft and context first. If a missing fact materially changes the recommendation, ask a focused question or make the uncertainty visible. Do not make routine wording choices depend on clarification.

Preserve the author's intent, audience, language, tone, facts, and requested length. Use this method to organize reasoning; do not force a consulting memo onto a personal note, a narrative, or a task with a required format.

Choose the requested mode:

- **Draft:** Turn the available material into a finished piece.
- **Rewrite:** Improve structure and expression while preserving meaning and qualifications.
- **Critique:** Identify consequential reasoning problems and show specific repairs. Do not rewrite the whole piece unless requested.

## Find the answer before polishing sentences

Formulate the reader's central question and the best-supported answer in one sentence. Make the answer a proposition, recommendation, or conclusion rather than a topic label. If the evidence does not resolve the question, state what can be concluded and what remains undecided.

Build from the material upward: facts and observations support findings; related findings support a broader conclusion. Present the result downward: answer, supporting points, then detail. An early draft of the answer is provisional. Revise it when the evidence requires a narrower or different claim.

Distinguish facts, interpretations, assumptions, and recommendations. Preserve sources, quantities, dates, and material caveats. Do not turn an aspiration into a forecast, correlation into causation, or a hypothesis into an established result. If the user requests persuasion, strengthen the reasoning rather than the certainty of unsupported claims.

## Build a coherent argument

For each major point, identify what the reader will need next: a reason, an explanation, evidence, or an implementation detail. Supply that material beneath the point.

Check the structure in both directions:

- **Vertical:** Does each parent point follow from the material beneath it? Does each supporting item help establish that point?
- **Horizontal:** Do sibling points answer the same question at a comparable level? Have reasons, actions, examples, and outcomes been accidentally mixed?

Use a synthesis sentence that says what the supporting points mean. An announcement that several considerations exist does not supply that synthesis. Use as many supporting points as the reasoning needs; do not invent a third reason to complete a pattern.

Where a set claims to divide a whole, test for MECE: mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive within the stated scope. Remove double counting and check for missing relevant parts. Related arguments can share dependencies; do not claim complete independence or exhaustive coverage without justification.

Choose an order that helps the argument: sequence for a process, components for a system, or a stated priority for comparable options. If one premise depends on another, preserve the dependency. Make the connection between premises and conclusion explicit rather than presenting a loose list as a proof.

## Give only the context the reader needs

Use Situation–Complication–Question to clarify the opening:

- **Situation:** The relevant starting context the reader can recognize.
- **Complication:** The change, obstacle, or tension that makes a response necessary.
- **Question:** The issue the document resolves.

The answer resolves that question. These are planning prompts, not mandatory headings or a requirement to ask a rhetorical question in the finished text. For a brief email, lead directly with the answer. For an unfamiliar or contested issue, a short context-setting introduction may come first. Avoid a long account of how the author reached the conclusion.

## Write the finished piece

Use concrete subjects, active verbs, and specific claims. Replace abstract topic headings with informative statements when headings help. Keep the tone appropriate to the relationship; structural clarity can be warm, diplomatic, or technical.

Place evidence next to the claim it supports. Keep material risks and limitations where they affect the decision. Move supporting detail to an appendix only when the main argument remains understandable without it.

For an action request, make the requested decision clear. Include an owner, timing, or success measure only when provided or explicitly proposed; do not invent commitments. Scale the presentation to the medium: a short email may need two paragraphs, a memo may need sections, and a slide storyline may need a sequence of claim-based titles.

Deliver the requested writing first. Provide an outline, critique, explanation of changes, or framework labels only when requested or necessary to resolve a material ambiguity. This skill organizes content; it does not authorize sending, publishing, or changing external documents.

## Review the logic before delivering

Read only the opening and main points: do they communicate the answer and a coherent case? Then check whether the evidence supports the strength of the language, whether a serious counterpoint changes the conclusion, and whether the requested action follows. Repair the argument before shortening sentences. Do not hide a reasoning gap with confident wording.

### Original miniature example

Suppose a draft reports a prototype demonstration, favorable comments, and a proposed store rollout. The material contains no store trial.

An appropriate rewrite might begin:

> Run a supervised store pilot before deciding on rollout. The demonstration supports testing the concept further, but it does not establish reliable performance in a live aisle. The pilot should examine obstacle handling and staff intervention before an expansion decision.

The recommendation matches the evidence and makes the next decision clear. Do not insert invented trial results, approval, dates, or performance targets to make it sound more complete.
