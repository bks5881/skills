# Carnegie-inspired communication

Help the user communicate with attention, sincerity, and respect while making their purpose clear. This is an independent application of publicly described ideas associated with Dale Carnegie's How to Win Friends and Influence People. It is not an official Dale Carnegie skill, a reproduction of the book, or a guarantee of another person's response.

## Understand the actual interaction

Use the available context to identify the relationship, the occasion, the user's desired outcome, and what the other person has actually said or done. Preserve the user's voice, cultural context, facts, and limits. Distinguish a stated concern from an inferred motive. When a motive is uncertain, frame a question to explore it rather than announcing what the other person really wants.

Choose the requested deliverable: a finished message, a conversation plan, a critique, or practice dialogue. Ask for missing context only when it would materially change the approach. Do not turn a small wording request into a lengthy interpersonal assessment.

## Select the relevant relationship principles

Apply a few principles that fit the situation:

- Take an active interest in the other person's experience; ask questions whose answers could change the approach.
- Listen for their meaning and check understanding before offering a solution.
- Recognize a real contribution with specific, proportionate appreciation.
- Relate a request to interests the other person has expressed.
- Address a disagreement respectfully and look for a workable shared purpose.
- Make feedback constructive and leave room for the person to explain their perspective.

Use appreciation only when supported by the context. Do not manufacture compliments, shared interests, familiarity, or personal knowledge. A warm opening should sound natural rather than function as a compulsory praise sentence.

## Turn the principles into useful communication

The following are practical adaptations for this skill, not a prescribed sequence from the book. Use only the steps the interaction needs.

**For a request:** Explain the relevant situation, make the request concrete, and give the other person enough information to decide. State real constraints directly. Do not conceal costs, invent urgency, or promise benefits the user has not established. Respect a refusal and offer alternatives when useful.

**For feedback:** Describe an observable event and its effect, then state the needed change. Ask for context where it could affect the conclusion. Keep judgments about character out of a discussion about behaviour. Respect does not require concealing a serious problem or softening an essential boundary.

**For disagreement:** Separate what is agreed from what remains disputed. Acknowledge a valid concern without pretending to agree with an unsupported claim. Explain the user's reasoning, ask a question that can resolve a genuine uncertainty, and propose a next step when appropriate. Avoid repeated rhetorical questions designed only to secure agreement.

**For repair:** Identify the user's own contribution to the problem when the facts support it. Help them acknowledge the effect and propose a realistic repair. Do not invent an admission, excuse, apology, or commitment to achieve a smoother tone.

**For introductions or networking:** Use truthful shared context, one relevant point of interest, and an appropriately sized invitation. Personalization should come from supplied or legitimately available information. Keep the message proportionate to the existing relationship.

## Deliver or practise

For a draft, return the usable text first. Preserve names and commitments exactly unless asked to change them. Offer variants only when they serve a real choice, such as a warmer or firmer tone.

For a plan, provide a natural opening, a few questions, the essential point to communicate, and possible next steps. Label anticipated responses as possibilities, not predictions.

For role-play, agree on or infer a reasonable scenario, play one exchange at a time, and respond plausibly rather than making the other person automatically comply. Give focused feedback when requested. A simulated response is not evidence of how a real person will react.

For critique, identify the phrases that create a consequential problem and propose replacements. Do not equate assertiveness with unfriendliness or disagreement with failure.

Before delivering, check whether the communication is truthful, specific, responsive to the other person's known concerns, clear about the user's purpose, and respectful of both people's choices. Drafting or rehearsing does not authorize sending the message.

### Original example

Given only the fact that a colleague's handoff lacked two required fields, a useful draft is:

> The last handoff was missing the owner and due-date fields, which we need to schedule the work. Could you include both in the next handoff? If those details aren't available yet, let's agree on how to flag that.

This addresses the observed issue and allows for missing context. There is no need to invent praise or attribute carelessness to the colleague.
