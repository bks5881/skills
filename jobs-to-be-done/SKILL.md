# Jobs to Be Done

Investigate the progress people seek in particular circumstances, including functional, emotional, and social dimensions. This is an independent application of the Christensen Institute's public Jobs to Be Done material. The research workflow and examples below are original adaptations.

## Anchor the assignment

Identify the decision the research should inform: whom to serve, why people switch, why a product is ignored, or which experience to improve. Preserve the user's product ambition and scope. Use available interviews, observations, transaction records, and direct user statements before asking for more context.

Distinguish the person using the product, the person choosing it, the person paying, and anyone able to block adoption. Do not assume they share one job. Define a useful episode or situation rather than relying only on demographic categories.

## Investigate real decisions

If designing research, recruit people with a relevant recent experience, including people who chose alternatives or decided against a purchase. The relevant window depends on how frequently the decision occurs; do not impose a universal interview count or recency rule.

Ask for a concrete account: what happened, what triggered a search, what they used before, which options they considered, what delayed them, who else mattered, and what happened after the choice. Seek specifics before supplying an explanation. Avoid pitching the concept midway through discovery or treating a hypothetical willingness to buy as a completed purchase.

Explore forces supporting and resisting a change: dissatisfaction with the current situation, appeal of a different outcome, concerns about the new option, and reasons to retain the familiar approach. Treat these as prompts for inquiry, not answers to fill in automatically.

## Synthesize with traceable evidence

For each relevant episode, record the circumstance, desired progress, actual alternatives, decision process, outcome, and source. Include workarounds and doing nothing when they were real options. Keep observed actions, participant explanations, and the analyst's interpretation separate.

Form a job statement in plain language: in this circumstance, the person wants to make this progress so they can achieve this outcome. Use a template only if it improves clarity. A job should explain a decision beyond restating the product feature or an abstract wish everyone shares.

Compare episodes for recurring patterns and meaningful differences. Explain which participants support a pattern and which contradict it. Do not convert a few interviews into population percentages, fabricate quotes, or generalize the user's own preference into market demand. If data are absent, call the result a job hypothesis and provide a way to investigate it.

## Connect learning to a decision

Explain which part of the experience could help people make the desired progress and what would have to change for them to adopt it. Distinguish a promising opportunity from proof that the proposed solution will work. Recommend research or a prototype that can discriminate between competing interpretations.

Deliver the requested interview guide, synthesis, or opportunity brief. Scale the output to the evidence. Include the consequential uncertainty and next decision; do not invent a full persona or market segment to make the deliverable look complete. Contacting participants or publishing findings requires authorization separate from preparing the work.

### Original example

A shopper asking for a robot cart could be seeking less physical effort, more independence, or faster shopping. Those are competing job hypotheses until relevant episodes support them. Ask about their last difficult shopping trip and what they actually did to manage it before selecting one explanation.
