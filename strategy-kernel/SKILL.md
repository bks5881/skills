# Strategy kernel

Help the user explain the central challenge, choose an approach to it, and coordinate actions that make the approach work. This is an independent application of Richard Rumelt's public strategy writing. The working instructions below are original adaptations.

## Establish the decision and evidence

Identify the organization or product, the decision-maker, the desired outcome, the time horizon, and constraints that materially affect the choice. Separate firm constraints from preferences. Preserve the user's objective; challenge an assumption without silently substituting a different goal.

Use supplied operating data, customer evidence, competitive information, and resource commitments. Verify external claims when they depend on current conditions. Mark assumptions and missing information. An attractive narrative is not a substitute for evidence about what is happening.

## Build or inspect the kernel

**Diagnosis:** Explain what makes progress difficult and why that obstacle matters. Distinguish a symptom, such as weak retention, from possible causes. Consider a competing explanation when it would imply a different strategy. State how uncertain the diagnosis is and what evidence could change it.

**Guiding policy:** Describe the overall approach chosen to address that diagnosis. Make the implied choices explicit: where effort will concentrate, which advantage or mechanism is expected to help, and which attractive alternatives will receive less attention. A numerical target alone does not explain an approach.

**Coherent actions:** Identify the commitments and linked moves needed to implement the policy. Check whether they reinforce one another or compete for the same resources. Identify a dependency whose failure would undermine the whole approach. Do not fill in owners, budgets, capabilities, or dates as if they were already agreed.

## Compare choices before polishing the story

When the direction is open, compare materially different approaches against the diagnosis, available resources, consequential risks, and the user's priorities. Explain why the recommended approach is preferable and what it gives up. Use numerical scores only when the criteria and inputs justify them.

When the user has selected a direction, work within it and surface relevant tradeoffs. Do not force a new strategy exercise for a bounded implementation task. If a critical assumption remains unresolved, provide a conditional recommendation and a targeted test rather than presenting a guess as a final decision.

## Make execution and learning concrete

Propose an achievable next milestone and the evidence needed to assess it. Distinguish results, such as repeat adoption, from activity counts, such as meetings held. State a review trigger when new information could change the approach. Do not automatically prescribe a fixed planning horizon or a large governance process.

Deliver a concise strategy or critique with the diagnosis, policy, linked actions, major tradeoff, and unresolved assumptions visible. For an existing plan, identify the consequential gaps rather than merely renaming its sections. Preparing a strategy does not authorize spending, personnel changes, or external commitments.

### Original example

For a shopping robot, a proposal to start in one store format could be a useful policy if evidence shows that variation between stores is the main deployment obstacle. If the real obstacle is poor shopper demand, standardizing the store environment addresses a different problem. The diagnosis must justify the choice.
