# IDEO-inspired product design

An independent adaptation of IDEO and IDEO.org's public methods, not an official IDEO skill or access to its internal practice. This file is self-contained. Source links and method-to-task mappings appear below under Public methods and provenance; consult them when selecting a method.

IDEO describes an iterative practice combining human desirability, technical feasibility, and economic viability. Its public methods connect challenge framing, research, synthesis, idea generation, prototypes, and feedback. Use these as complementary activities rather than a rigid approval sequence.

## Begin with the assignment and available evidence

Carry forward the user's actual objective, preferred tools, constraints, and explicit features. Reframing should open useful possibilities while preserving that intent. A request for an ambitious concept is not permission to quietly substitute a less ambitious product.

Identify the intended people, situation, and outcome. Use supplied interviews, observations, direct user feedback, existing products, and connected sources where available. Distinguish:

- **Observed or stated:** a traceable behavior, source, or direct user preference.
- **Interpretation:** an explanation of what that evidence might mean.
- **Assumption:** something plausible but not investigated.
- **Design proposal:** an intended response, still open to testing.

Never invent interviews, field observations, participant quotes, demand statistics, or successful tests. A user's preference establishes that person's preference; it does not establish market demand. When working without field access, proceed with explicit assumptions and a focused research plan. Do not make research access an artificial blocker to authorized concept work.

## Frame the opportunity

Express the opportunity as a short outcome-oriented question, often “How might we …?” Scope it to a person and context, with enough freedom for different mechanisms. Check whether it can produce genuinely different answers. Keep firm user requirements beside the question rather than erasing them through abstraction.

Consider the surrounding journey: preparation, first use, normal operation, handoffs, exceptions, and return or maintenance. Include affected people beyond the purchaser when relevant, such as staff, bystanders, caregivers, cleaners, or repairers. Select the few moments that most affect the requested outcome.

Translate evidence into a need and opportunity. Keep observation separate from interpretation. If a proposed insight rests on an assumption, label it a hypothesis.

## Explore materially different concepts

Develop a small, useful spread of concepts before polishing one. Change the operating mechanism, interaction, arrangement, or service model; color changes and extra features alone do not constitute distinct concepts. Use analogous activities or unusual combinations when they expose a new direction.

For each serious concept, describe who benefits, how the critical moment works, what must be true, and a consequential tradeoff. A speculative direction can remain valuable even when its enabling technology is immature. Identify the dependency and horizon rather than dismissing the user's ambition.

Compare concepts against the user's stated priorities, evidence of desirability, technical dependencies, and operational viability. Include accessibility, physical safety, privacy, maintenance, or environmental consequences where they materially change the design. Avoid invented numerical scores, cost estimates, or universal checklists.

Select a direction and explain the reasoning. Where the user has already selected a direction, explore alternatives within that direction and continue; do not force an unnecessary choice or approval round.

## Prototype to answer a question

Name the important uncertainty and the decision it affects. Choose the smallest useful prototype for that question. A storyboard may explore a handoff; an appearance model may explore form and perceived intent; a working mechanism or physical mock-up may investigate reach or load.

Match fidelity to the question and the requested deliverable. If the user asks for Blender, CAD, or another medium, use it when available. Use a complementary test plan for questions that medium cannot answer. Produce the authorized artifact rather than stopping at a process description.

For physical or robotic concepts, distinguish:

- Appearance and spatial-layout studies.
- Scripted interaction or motion demonstrations.
- Measured mechanical, control, or behavioral performance.

A render does not prove manufacturability, and keyframed motion does not prove autonomy, collision avoidance, stable loads, safe contact, or reliable grasping. Do not add certification claims or tested ratings without evidence. Report material limitations concisely, in the context of the relevant design decision.

## Learn and revise

Define a practical test: the question, relevant people and context, task, observations to capture, and the result that would change the design. Prefer observed behavior over praise. Label any proposed thresholds as design targets, not established standards or achieved results.

When real feedback exists, record what changed and why. When testing has not happened, deliver the prototype with a concrete next experiment; do not present the experiment as completed. A simulation or desk review is its own evidence category.

## Deliver the useful outcome

Scale the output to the task. A quick concept may need a clear proposition, a visual, its major tradeoff, and one next test. A broader engagement may warrant a brief, journey, concept comparison, assumption register, and prototype plan. Avoid filling every possible template by default.

Make evidence, reasoning, proposed behavior, and unresolved questions easy to distinguish. Keep reusable process material in the skill, and project-specific requirements or decisions in the project. Preserve existing artifacts when exploring another direction.

### Applying the methods honestly

For a desk-based concept request, identify direct user statements, separate assumptions, explore different architectures, and make an appearance or interaction prototype. Record a specific future experiment where field evidence is missing.

For a research-supported project, connect the source material to needs, concept decisions, and observed prototype results. Revise the frame if the evidence shows that the original interpretation was incomplete.

For an ambitious future product, keep the desired experience visible while identifying the technologies, infrastructure, or operational changes that would enable it. Stage prototypes by the uncertainty they investigate. A staged prototype is not a reason to redefine the ultimate goal without the user's agreement.
