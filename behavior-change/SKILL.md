# Behaviour change with COM-B

Investigate a particular behaviour using capability, opportunity, and motivation. This is an independent adaptation of public work by Susan Michie, Maartje van Stralen, Robert West, and UCL. COM-B is a framework for inquiry; applying its labels does not establish an individual cause or guarantee an intervention's effect.

## Define the behaviour and purpose

Specify who would do what, in which situation, and when. Replace a broad aim such as improving engagement with an observable action relevant to the user's goal. Confirm the intended benefit and whose interests it serves. Preserve the ability to decline or choose an alternative.

Use direct feedback, observation, workflow data, and relevant research. Separate observed behaviour from a stated intention and from an inferred explanation. Treat incomplete information as a research need rather than evidence that people lack motivation.

## Investigate the COM-B conditions

- **Capability:** Physical ability and psychological resources such as understanding, knowledge, and skills.
- **Opportunity:** Physical conditions and social surroundings that enable or obstruct the action.
- **Motivation:** Deliberate goals and evaluations as well as automatic processes such as habit and emotion.

These conditions can influence one another. Check the plausible explanations rather than forcing one category to be the sole cause. Ask questions or observe tasks that distinguish a skill gap, an environmental obstacle, and a motivational barrier.

Record the proposed barrier, supporting evidence, competing explanation, and confidence in the interpretation. Consider differences between situations and groups. Do not diagnose an individual's mental health, assign a personality type, or infer a private motive from usage logs alone.

## Match changes to the evidence

Generate interventions that address the identified barrier. The following are application examples, not a universal mapping: clearer practice may help a skill gap; changing the environment may remove a practical obstacle; revising prompts may address a missed cue. Check what each intervention assumes and what burdens it introduces.

Do not default to reminders, rewards, or persuasive copy when the action is physically inaccessible or incompatible with the workflow. Consider whether the requested behaviour itself needs redesign. Keep essential information and exits visible; an intervention should help people act on an informed choice.

Compare candidate changes by the mechanism they target, expected benefit, feasibility, acceptability, cost, and distribution of benefits or burdens where relevant. Use measured inputs or label estimates. Explain the consequential tradeoff without creating an elaborate scorecard for a small change.

## Test a causal explanation

Specify the target action, context, proposed change, observation method, and comparison where practical. Define the result that would support or weaken the barrier hypothesis. Keep implementation checks separate from outcomes: delivering a reminder does not show it helped.

Use proposed thresholds as design choices with a rationale. Do not claim measured effect sizes, statistical power, or successful behaviour change without actual data. Consider unintended effects that could reverse the intended benefit. If the user asks for implementation, produce the authorized artifact and explain what remains to be tested.

Deliver a concise diagnosis and a proportionate test plan. Distinguish evidence, hypothesis, intervention, and result. This skill does not authorize contacting participants, changing live systems, or enrolling people in an experiment.

### Original example

If shoppers abandon robot-cart pairing, investigate whether they understand the steps, can physically reach the controls, have room and time to pair, or prefer not to use the cart. A more enthusiastic welcome message would test only a narrow part of those possibilities.
