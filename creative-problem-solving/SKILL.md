# Creative problem solving

Expand the range of useful possibilities before selecting a direction. This is an independent application of the Creative Education Foundation's public account of Creative Problem Solving, rooted in the Osborn-Parnes tradition. The practical prompts and examples below are original adaptations.

## Clarify what should change

Identify the desired outcome, the people involved, the current approach, and the obstacle. Keep the user's explicit requirements visible. Distinguish a real constraint from a convention or an untested assumption. Reframe the challenge as an open question that can support different mechanisms without erasing the assignment.

If the user asks for a more ambitious concept, increase the distance explored rather than quietly reducing the goal to present capabilities. Distinguish ideas that can be prototyped now from ideas dependent on future technology or changed infrastructure.

## Generate before selecting

Separate divergent and convergent work. During generation, seek variety and develop the promising implication of an unusual idea before judging it. During selection, apply explicit criteria. Move between these activities as needed; do not confuse deferring judgment with accepting every idea.

Use a few prompts appropriate to the challenge. These are working prompts for this skill, not a reproduction of a proprietary exercise set:

- Change who performs a task, where it happens, or when it happens.
- Remove an assumed component and find another way to deliver its benefit.
- Borrow a mechanism from a different activity and explain why it might transfer.
- Reverse a dependency or combine capabilities that are usually separate.
- Make a deliberate extreme version, then extract the useful mechanism.

Vary the mechanism, arrangement, interaction, or service model. Renaming the same concept, changing its color, or adding superficial features does not establish meaningful diversity. Produce enough range to support the user's decision rather than chasing an arbitrary idea count.

## Turn ideas into coherent concepts

For each serious candidate, explain the experience, how the mechanism delivers the benefit, what must be true, and a consequential tradeoff. Combine ideas only when they reinforce a coherent experience; avoid a feature bundle without an organizing purpose.

Separate familiar improvements, architectural changes, and more speculative directions when that helps comparison. These are relative descriptions, not verified novelty rankings. Do not claim an idea is unprecedented, patented, feasible, or market-validated because it was generated in a brainstorming session.

## Select while preserving useful novelty

Compare candidates against the user's outcome and constraints. Consider potential benefit, meaningful difference, dependencies, feasibility horizon, and what can be learned next. Explain judgment-based choices rather than inventing numerical scores or costs.

If the user has selected a direction, explore alternatives within it. If an unusual option is rejected, explain the specific obstacle and whether a modified version could preserve its useful feature. Reopen generation when every option shares the same failing assumption.

## Make the next move tangible

Deliver the requested concepts, visual, narrative, or prototype when the medium is available. Add a targeted experiment for the most consequential uncertainty rather than stopping at a process explanation. A short storyboard may test an interaction; a mechanism mock-up may test motion or reach. Choose according to the question.

State which ideas are proposals and which claims have supporting evidence. Do not invent research sessions, participant reactions, or successful tests. Keep project-specific choices in the project, and preserve previous artifacts when exploring another direction.

### Original example

For a cart that fetches groceries, explore a mobile arm, shelf-to-cart transfer, and a coordinated picking service as different mechanisms. If the user requires the cart itself to retrieve products, retain that requirement and investigate the first two more deeply; the service alternative can remain a comparison rather than replacing the requested experience.
